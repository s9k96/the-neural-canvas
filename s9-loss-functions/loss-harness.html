Four Ways to Lie About a Loss

Between the model's output and the one number the optimiser pushes down there are four lines of code. Every bug that lives in them is silent — none of them raise, and three of them make the loss look better. Flip the switches and watch the number you would have been staring at during training move the wrong way.

00The four lines

# the whole of it
hidden = model(tokens)                     # [B, T, D]
logits = output_head(hidden)               # [B, T, V]  <- V/D times larger
loss   = cross_entropy(
    logits[:, :-1].reshape(-1, V),     # drop the last position: nothing follows it
    tokens[:, 1:].reshape(-1),          # drop the first token: nothing predicts it
)

Everything on this page was measured by s9_loss_harness.py on a real 4-layer decoder with the real frozen Sarvam-1 tokenizer this course's pipeline uses — 68,096 tokens, sha256 …, the same hash S6 pins and S7 measured against — over real Hindi, Telugu and code documents from S6's committed corpus. No number below is illustrative.

01The four switches

Two of these are reduction choices — same model, different arithmetic on the way to the scalar — so the page recomputes them live from the — per-token losses baked into it. The other two are training choices: get them wrong and you train a different model, so those are measured constants from separate runs and the panel says so.

02Print the strings, not the integers

The assignment says it twice, so it is worth saying again here: a target shift in the wrong direction produces a beautiful loss curve and no exception. You cannot see it in a wall of token ids. You can see it instantly in the strings.

03The anchor: an untrained model sits at ln(V)

The cheapest sanity check in training. Before a single step, the model has no reason to prefer any token, so it should be as unsure as a uniform draw from the vocabulary — a loss of ln(V) and a perplexity of V. If a run does not start near there, the target alignment is wrong and nothing downstream is worth reading.

It does not land exactly on ln(V), and the size of the miss is itself predictable. For logits drawn as z ~ N(0, σ²) the expected loss is ln(V) + σ²/2: the log-sum-exp picks up ln E[e^z] = σ²/2 while the true-token term averages to zero. So the excess is not slack in the check — it is the logit spread, and measuring σ predicts it.

04The third bill, and it arrives last

Attention's bill grows with context. The logits bill grows with tokens times vocabulary, and it lands in the final layer after all the clever attention work is done. Drag the context out and watch one intermediate tensor — whose only purpose is to be collapsed into a single scalar — outgrow every accelerator you can buy.

Tied against untied, and the door that is closed to V5

05Same loss, a fraction of the memory

Chunking changes nothing about what the model learns. It computes the logits for a block of tokens, takes their loss, throws the logits away, and recomputes them in the backward pass when it needs them — a little extra arithmetic for a great deal of memory. Drag the chunk size and watch the peak logit tensor fall.

Measured, not just calculated

06One extra head, and the gap that grows

A second head on the same trunk predicting token t+2. The losses simply add. Both heads fall together and head 2 stays worse — but the part worth reporting is that the gap widens rather than closing.

head 1 → t+1 head 2 → t+2 held-out batch, never trained on

On a 5–11 nat axis the two curves sit on top of each other, which is itself the finding: the extra head costs almost nothing in loss. The quantity this section is about is their difference, so it gets its own axis rather than a second scale on the chart above.

At the start the two heads are equally bad, because the model has learned nothing: it is predicting roughly the unigram distribution, and that distribution is the same one step out as two. t+1 and t+2 only become different questions once there is context to condition on. The gap is not present at initialisation and then eroded — it is created by learning, and it grows as the model gets better at what head 1 is asked to do.

The mechanism: head 2 never gets to condition on how t+1 actually resolved. Head 1 predicts one step into a distribution; head 2 predicts two and must marginalise over the token in between. Its irreducible entropy is genuinely higher, so its loss has a higher floor. The gap is information, not an optimisation failure — a perfectly trained pair of heads would still show it. Which is why the acceptance rate, not the head count, is what decides whether multi-token prediction pays.
